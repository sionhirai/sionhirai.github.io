Who leaves, and why

Canterra loses about one in six employees a year. Leadership asked three things: who is leaving, why, and who to talk to first. Scroll to walk through how we answered.

Data
4,410 employee records, 17 predictors
Models
Logistic regression vs. decision tree
Result
Test AUC 0.73 · 2.1× lift
Tools
Python · scikit-learn · statsmodels
01 · WHO LEAVES

Attrition is front-loaded

Overall, 16% of employees left. But 46% of people in their first two years of work left, and so did 33% of those in their first year with a new manager.

Frequent travelers and single employees also left more often. These are one-at-a-time comparisons, so the models had to test which ones hold up together.

02 · THE TRAP

The file repeats itself

The 4,410 rows are really 1,470 profiles, each appearing three times with identical details and outcomes.

A normal random split scatters copies of the same profile across training and test. Here, 96% of test rows had a twin in training, so the model would be graded on people it had already seen.

03 · THE FIX

Split by profile, not by row

We kept every row but moved each profile's three copies together. 294 profiles went into a test set we touched once, at the very end.

Cross-validation used the same grouping, and every prep step (filling 73 blank cells, scaling) was redone inside each fold so nothing leaked.

04 · THE MODELS

The simpler model ranked better

The logistic regression beat a tuned decision tree in cross-validation (AUC 0.741 vs. 0.674) and on the untouched test set (0.727 vs. 0.666).

We still kept the tree as a companion: anyone can follow its if-then rules without statistics.

05 · DOES IT WORK?

Ranking people by risk

On the test set, the riskiest fifth of employees left at 41%, compared with 5% for the safest fifth.

Flagging the top 30% for a check-in reaches 62% of the people who leave. One in three flagged employees leaves, twice the base rate.

06 · WHAT TO DO

Two levers managers control

Experience and marital status can't be changed, but travel and satisfaction can. In the model, an employee with frequent travel and low satisfaction scores sits at 40% risk. Cutting travel and lifting both scores one point drops that to 14%, below the check-in cutoff.

Our recommendations: a first-two-years retention program, a check-in whenever someone gets a new manager, and less reliance on frequent travel. The risk score is for prioritizing conversations, never for pay or promotion decisions.

07 Limits we stated up front

  • These are associations, not proven causes. The what-if numbers are what the model implies.
  • The test set has only 47 leaving profiles, so the accuracy figures are approximate.
  • Accuracy is moderate (test AUC 0.73). The model prioritizes; it does not predict individuals.
